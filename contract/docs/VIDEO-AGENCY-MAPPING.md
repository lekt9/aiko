# What Makes Agency Actually Work? — implications for Contract

Source: Carlos E. Perez, “What Makes Agency Actually Work?”,
`https://youtu.be/53Y3SYR5vTU`, reviewed on 2026-07-20. Downloaded audio,
captions, and provider metadata are local research inputs and are intentionally
excluded from version control.

## Central claim

Agency is not “a loop that executes.” It is an ensemble of coupled capacities. Perception must touch reality; action must create and observe consequences; governance must preserve authored values across time; relationships must transmit quality without capture; and development must be able to change the loop architecture itself.

The decisive distinction is visible under stress. A formally compliant system and a genuinely grounded agent can look identical during normal operation. When conditions depart from the specification, the grounded agent senses, interprets, and adapts; the hollow system repeats or escalates enforcement.

## The five families

### 1. Perception — quality ground

The agent must be affected by reality before acting. Metrics and reports are representations of quality, not quality itself. Its distinctions must be capable of emerging from encounter rather than being limited to pre-supplied categories.

Contract implication: evidence cannot mean only “another ledger row agrees.” At least some evidence must terminate in an independent reality anchor: an executed test, observed browser state, external API result, human judgment, physical measurement, or economic consequence. A graph made only of mutually validating contracts is coherent but ungrounded.

### 2. Action — causally effective loops

Effective action is:

```text
act → perceive result → understand result → adjust → act again
```

Replacing the perception-and-understanding phase with commands destroys adaptability. Reach must not exceed the ability to observe consequences. Meta-system overhead must match operational load: too little produces under-governance; too much produces a system where everyone watches and nobody works.

Contract implication: a contract should contain action with boundaries and a verifier, not micromanage every action step. Every permitted effect needs an observable consequence at the same reach. Verification cost must remain proportional to execution risk.

### 3. Governance — coherent selfhood through time

Values may be authored from contact with what matters or installed as decorative specifications. Purpose is a stable attractor, not a slogan. Evaluative sovereignty includes the ability to question the evaluation criteria themselves. Bad-news channels must reach governance or regeneration becomes impossible.

Contract implication: the user owns the root purpose and frozen boundaries. Contract supplies durable memory, evidence, and enforcement, but must not pretend to originate “better.” Failure events are first-class evidence and cannot be filtered out to preserve a green status. Slower governance contracts should own the targets of faster operational contracts.

### 4. Relating — the coupling spine

Boundaries need selective permeability: too rigid means isolation; too permeable means capture. Grounding integrity is the continuing dialectic between internal knowledge and external encounter. Collective capacity emerges when participants retain autonomy inside shared containment. Communication must preserve enough fidelity that living practice does not decay into dead rules.

Contract implication: `parent_id`, `contract:<id>`, hooks, event buses, evaluators, and evidence lineage are the coupling substrate. Their job is not merely to connect nodes, but to preserve provenance, independence, authority, and semantic fidelity across edges. Circular confirmation and duplicated evidence must be detected as loss of grounding, not counted as confidence.

### 5. Development — changing the architecture

Adaptation changes what runs through existing loops. Development changes which loops exist, how they are coupled, and which tensions they manage. Forced complexity can produce a more elaborate but less alive system. Authored development is drawn toward a sensed better configuration, rather than imposed only by crisis.

Contract implication: retrying an action is adaptation. Revising the graph is development. Contract needs an explicit, human-authorized topology-change path: propose a new evaluator, replace an obsolete metric, split a conflicted loop, revoke a compromised anchor, or alter arbitration. The machinery may propose structural change but should not silently rewrite its own governing purpose.

## What Contract is for

Contract should not be a command engine that turns a goal into a rigid checklist. It should be the durable coupling and governance layer around an intelligent actor:

```text
human-authored purpose
        ↓
reality anchors → perception contracts
        ↓                 ↑
bounded action → observed consequences
        ↓                 ↑
independent audit + counter-metrics
        ↓
slower governance and arbitration
        ↓
human-approved topology development
        └──────────────→ next cycle
```

The language model or human remains the part that interprets ambiguity, tastes the result, and proposes action. Contract records commitments, resolves dependencies, preserves lineage, runs deterministic checks, surfaces failure, and prevents the faster loops from quietly weakening the rules that judge them.

## Current strengths

- Durable user-authored declarations.
- Bounded deterministic actions and checks.
- Dependency and containment edges.
- Append-only activity and evidence history.
- Signed outcomes and environment identity.
- Invalidation, retry, caching, and scheduled recurrence.
- Trust concepts for provenance, independence, frozen governance, counter-metrics, temporal validity, circularity, revocation, and rollback.

## Gaps exposed by the video

1. **A passing check is not automatically a quality ground.** The verifier can prove that a command returned zero while the real outcome remains poor.
2. **The ledger is observable but not yet perceptually legible.** People need a live graph showing actions, anchors, conflicts, stale evidence, vetoes, retries, and which node currently owns the target.
3. **Verifier independence must affect confidence.** Several checks derived from one source are one witness, not several.
4. **Failure must travel upward.** Bad-news paths need priority and must not be suppressed by cached success.
5. **Reach needs matching monitoring.** Network, filesystem, payment, deployment, and human-impact actions need consequence sensors at the same scope.
6. **Loop speed needs explicit ownership.** Fast task loops, slower audit loops, and slow purpose/governance loops should not mutate one another symmetrically.
7. **Topology change needs a governed verb.** Retry and invalidation are insufficient when the loop architecture itself is wrong.
8. **Human qualitative judgment needs a typed place.** Not as an omnipotent override, but as attributed, scoped evidence with authority and expiry.

## Recommended next architecture

Build a Contract agency graph with six explicit node roles:

1. `purpose` — human-authored root values and non-negotiable boundaries.
2. `sensor` — independent observations that touch the external world.
3. `action` — bounded causal work with declared reach.
4. `evaluator` — success criteria, counter-metrics, uncertainty, and independence class.
5. `governor` — arbitration, veto, expiry, revocation, rollback, and target ownership.
6. `development` — proposed topology changes requiring explicit authority.

Every resolved action should answer:

- What reality anchor did it touch?
- Which independent evaluator judged it?
- What counter-metric could veto it?
- Who owns the target?
- When does the evidence expire?
- How far did the action reach, and how was that reach observed?
- What failure travels upward if the check is wrong?
- Is the next change adaptation inside the loop or development of the graph?

That is the difference between Contract as a signed checklist and Contract as an agency substrate.
